The Case Against Plastic Bowls

Plastic scratches, holds odor, and gets replaced. Steel and stoneware do none of that — and over three years, they cost less.

Plastic is a fine material for many things. A pet bowl is not one of them. Every plastic bowl picks up micro-scratches the first time a cat's tongue or a kibble hits the surface, and those scratches multiply over weeks. They are too fine to see and too shallow to clean. What settles into them is not just food residue but the beginning of a film that scrubbing never quite removes. The bowl looks fine in daylight and harbors a smell only the cat notices.

Those scratches do not stay empty. In a warm kitchen, with a little water and a trace of saliva, they become a home for biofilm — a thin, sticky layer of bacteria that forms within a day or two. It is not dangerous in the way old water is dangerous, but it is why plastic develops that faint greasy feel and that particular odor even after washing. Glazed stoneware and steel resist this by not scratching. The difference between materials is the difference between washing a bowl and getting it clean.

Smell is the tell. A plastic bowl that has held wet food for a few months keeps the ghost of that food long after scrubbing, because the odor works into the surface. Cats notice before we do. Smell is their first sense, and a bowl that smells of last Tuesday's dinner, however faintly, can put them off eating or drinking. Stainless steel does not absorb odor, and glazed stoneware does not either, because both are sealed surfaces. What rinses off, rinses off.

The fix is not expensive. 316 stainless steel is the grade used in restaurant kitchens, chosen because it shrugs off acids and water. Glazed stoneware is the same idea in ceramic: a smooth, fired surface with no pores for odor or film to grip. Both go straight into the dishwasher, which is where dishwasher-safe earns its keep. A wash cycle runs hotter than a kitchen tap and dries harder, so what comes out is genuinely clean, not wiped clean. You do the work once a day.

The cost argument settles it. A cheap plastic bowl runs six to ten dollars and lasts maybe a year before it looks and smells wrong. A stoneware or steel bowl costs more up front — the Still Bowl is $45 — but it is the same bowl on day one and day one thousand. It does not fade or hold a patina of old dinner. You are paying for a bowl you will keep, and keeping it is cheaper by the third year.

Spend once on a sealed steel or stoneware bowl, and the three-year math quietly favors you.
